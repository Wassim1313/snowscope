    What 46 winters say about Aspen Snowmass

    Here is how the snow usually builds up at Aspen Snowmass (10,315 ft (3,144 m)), from 46 past winters.

    On 25 December the usual depth is 39 in (98 cm), with a normal range of 28 in (70 cm)–46 in (118 cm).

    Mid-February typically sees 58 in (148 cm), between 48 in (123 cm) and 70 in (178 cm) in a normal winter.

    March is the deepest month, with the cover peaking near 69 in (174 cm).

    A 50 cm base was there by 15 December in 78 % of winters, and by 1 January in 91 %.

    Best winter on record: 1983-84, peaking at 112 in (284 cm). Worst: 1980-81, which never passed 46 in (117 cm).

    The skiable season with at least 12 in (30 cm) typically runs early November to late May (202 days).

    Today's 7.1 in (18 cm) compares with a usual 17 in (42 cm) for 19 November.

    Aspen Snowmass: quick answers

    Is there snow in Aspen Snowmass at Christmas?

    Usually yes. The upper slopes hold about 39 in (98 cm) at Christmas, and 91 % of the past 46 winters had at least 20 in (50 cm) by 1 January. Today there is 26 in (67 cm).

    When is the best month to ski Aspen Snowmass?

    March: the snow peaks at about 69 in (174 cm) then. A base of 12 in (30 cm) or more usually lasts from early November to late May.

    How deep does the snow get at Aspen Snowmass?

    In a typical winter the upper-mountain snow peaks at about 69 in (174 cm) in late March. The deepest of 46 winters reached 112 in (284 cm) (1983-84), the thinnest 46 in (117 cm) (1980-81).

    When does Aspen Snowmass open for the 2026/27 season?

    The announced opening day is 26 November 2026; the last ski day is planned for 11 April 2027.

    How high is Aspen Snowmass?

    Its highest lift reaches 3,815 m; the lowest starts at 2,474 m (1,341 m of vertical).

    Is Aspen Snowmass good for advanced skiers?

    Yes: about 33 km of its graded pistes (28%) are difficult.

    About Aspen Snowmass

    About 120 km of pistes make Aspen Snowmass one of the ten biggest ski areas of US West (number 7). The ski area spans the mountain from 2,474 m up to 3,815 m. Buttermilk is the next resort, 6.9 km away to the east.

    Lifts and terrain

    The vertical drop is 1,341 m: a long top-to-bottom run that tests the legs. Even the lowest lift starts at 2,474 m, high enough for the bottom of the resort to keep cold snow longer than most. No other ski area within 50 km reaches as high as its 3,815 m. Its 19 lifts break down into 1 funicular, 2 gondolas, 12 chairlifts, 1 T-bar and 3 button lifts. You sit down on most of the lifts here, which suits children and snowboarders. The cabin lifts are sheltered on cold or windy days and also take walkers and sightseers up. Above the ski area rises Willoughby Mountain (4,004 m), 7.3 km to the south-west, about 189 m higher than the top lift.

    Pistes and who they suit

    In all, 107 marked runs cover roughly 120 km. Blue square runs make up the biggest part: 78 km, or 65%. Intermediate runs make up 65% of the length, the sweet spot for most holiday skiers. Experts can count on some 33 km of difficult runs.

    When the snow is good

    Count on about 28 weeks of solid cover (30 cm or more) halfway up the slopes, around 3,144 m in an ordinary winter, from mid-November to late May. For the best snow, aim for late March and early April. Spring skiers should know that mid-April usually has 150 cm, plenty for sunny spring days. A metre-deep snowpack is normal for around 19 weeks. At Christmas there was snow here in every winter, typically 98 cm, from 46 cm in a poor year to 145 cm in a big one. Year to year the snow is steady, with 109 cm to 205 cm in mid-February in most winters. A normal season tops out near 174 cm, around 30 March.
