    What 46 winters say about Mount Ashland

    Here is how the snow usually builds up at Mount Ashland (6,896 ft (2,102 m)), from 46 past winters.

    Christmas week typically brings 42 in (106 cm) of cover (11 in (29 cm) to 64 in (162 cm) in the middle half of winters).

    Mid-February typically sees 59 in (149 cm), between 26 in (66 cm) and 112 in (284 cm) in a normal winter.

    The deepest snow comes in February: about 74 in (189 cm) at the peak of a typical winter.

    A 50 cm base was there by 15 December in 52 % of winters, and by 1 January in 65 %.

    Best winter on record: 1982-83, peaking at 381 in (967 cm). Worst: 2013-14, which never passed 19 in (47 cm).

    A cover of 12 in (30 cm) or more usually lasts from late November to mid-April, about 152 days.

    This winter so far: 4.7 in (12 cm) on 27 November, against a usual 5.9 in (15 cm) for the date.

    Mount Ashland: quick answers

    Can you ski Mount Ashland at Christmas?

    Usually yes. The upper slopes hold about 42 in (106 cm) at Christmas, and 65 % of the past 46 winters had at least 20 in (50 cm) by 1 January. Today there is 11 in (28 cm).

    When is the best month to ski Mount Ashland?

    The deepest snow comes in February (about 74 in (189 cm)). For a sure base, late November to mid-April is the usual window with 12 in (30 cm) or more.

    How snowy is Mount Ashland?

    About 74 in (189 cm) at the peak of a normal winter (early March); between 19 in (47 cm) in the leanest winter (2013-14) and 381 in (967 cm) in the biggest (1982-83).

    When does Mount Ashland have the most snow?

    Aim for early January and mid-February; the cover is usually good from early December to early April.

    How high do the lifts go at Mount Ashland?

    The top is at 2,279 m and the base at 1,925 m.

    Does Mount Ashland have steep runs?

    Yes: about 6.2 km of its graded pistes (41%) are difficult.

    About Mount Ashland

    Neither huge nor tiny, Mount Ashland offers about 17 km of pistes (number 109 of 175 in US West). The ski area spans the mountain from 1,925 m up to 2,279 m.

    Pistes and who they suit

    Counting every graded piste, there are 57 runs and around 15 km to ski. The largest share is blue square, with 8.2 km (55%). Intermediate runs make up 55% of the length, the sweet spot for most holiday skiers. The steepest pistes add up to about 6.2 km.

    Heights, vertical and lifts

    354 m separate the highest and lowest lifts, runs of a moderate length. A high base at 1,925 m means the lowest runs rarely turn to slush in midwinter. The lift system counts 6 lifts: 5 chairlifts and 1 moving carpet. You sit down on most of the lifts here, which suits children and snowboarders. 1 moving carpet serves the beginner and children's areas. Named peaks within a few kilometres include Mount Ashland (2,296 m), Wagner Butte (2,211 m) and McDonald Peak (2,202 m).

    This week's snow

    Today there are about 28 cm of snow on the upper slopes of Mount Ashland. The last seven days brought 8 cm of new snow. More is on the way: about 9 cm of new snow is expected by Monday. An ordinary winter would have around 30 cm at mid-mountain by now, so this one is on track.

    The snow season, winter by winter

    Count on about 18 weeks of solid cover (30 cm or more) at mid-mountain (about 2,102 m) in an ordinary winter, from early December to early April. Winters vary a lot: in mid-February the depth has ranged from 8 cm to 515 cm in most years, so look at the snow before booking. For the holidays at the end of December, expect around 106 cm (snow lay here in nearly every winter); a snowy December gave 280 cm, a dry one 2 cm. Spring skiers should know that mid-April usually has 19 cm; by then it pays to ski early, before the sun softens it. A metre-deep snowpack is normal for around 13 weeks. Snow conditions have usually peaked in early January and mid-February. The snow is usually deepest around 7 March, with about 189 cm.
