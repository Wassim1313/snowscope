Your first day at Park City Mountain: a plan from first lift to last run

US WestGuidesPublished Monday 9 November 2026 · 21:44 UTCData of 9 November3 min read

Park City Mountain is so big that a first day can disappear in lift lines and wrong turns. Here is a simple plan for that day, plus what our snow history of 46 past winters (a model, not resort reports) says about where and when the snow is best.

Before you go: the size of it

Park City is the largest lift-served ski resort in the United States, about 6,700 acres with 41 lifts, since it was joined with Canyons by the Quicksilver Gondola in 2015. The top is at 10,026 ft (3,056 m) and the base at 6,900 ft (2,100 m), a vertical rise of 3,100 feet (945 m). The resort lies about 32 miles (51 km) east of Salt Lake City, so many visitors fly in and drive up the same day.

Do not try to ski it all. Pick one side of the mountain for the morning, and decide at lunch whether to cross to the other.

Morning: go up first, warm up high

Take the first lifts straight to the upper mountain, where the snow is deeper and colder. Our model gives the usual peak of the season as about 111 in (281 cm) near the top, around 9,977 ft (3,041 m), against about 71 in (180 cm) at mid-mountain, around 8,435 ft (2,571 m), and only about 42 in (106 cm) near the base. Warm up on a long groomed blue run up there, while the lower slopes are still hard from the night.

Late morning: follow the sun

As the sun climbs, slopes facing east and south soften first; north-facing runs keep their cold, dry snow longer. Intermediate skiers can link several bowls and ridges without dropping to the valley. If you came for steeper terrain, this is the time to try it, once you know how the snow feels.

Lunch and the afternoon

Eat early or late to avoid the noon rush. After lunch, if you have energy, cross the mountain by the Quicksilver Gondola and explore the other side; if not, stay with the runs you know. Plan your last run so you end near your car or bus: on a big mountain, the walk back can be long.

When to plan the trip

In our model the usual depth at mid-mountain is about 8 in (21 cm) in November, 26 in (67 cm) in December, 44 in (112 cm) in January, 55 in (140 cm) in February and 61 in (155 cm) in March, the deepest month, then 48 in (121 cm) in April. Near the top March and April keep about 91 in (230 cm) and 88 in (224 cm). In 9 of 10 winters, mid-mountain had at least 20 in (50 cm) for 15 weeks of the season.

The trend is down. Comparing the first 15 winters of the record with the last 15, the usual peak at mid-mountain fell from about 75 in (190 cm) to about 62 in (158 cm), and near the top from about 120 in (304 cm) to about 101 in (257 cm). Last winter, 2025-26, was the thinnest of the 46 at mid-mountain, with a peak of only about 31 in (79 cm). For the safest cover, go in February or March.

Staying and safety

Old Town Park City and the base villages have beds at every price, and the town is close enough to Salt Lake City to stay there instead and drive up. Beyond the resort boundary there is no patrol and avalanche terrain is close: read the daily Utah Avalanche Center forecast and carry a transceiver, probe and shovel.

Tips for skiers

  • Ski the upper mountain first thing: it holds about twice the snow of the base in our model.
  • Choose one side of the mountain per morning instead of crossing back and forth.
  • Book February or March for the most reliable cover.
  • Leaving the boundary? Read the Utah Avalanche Center forecast first.
