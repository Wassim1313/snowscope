The Sella Ronda from Val Gardena, leg by leg: four passes and the snow at each height

AlpsGuidesPublished Monday 9 November 2026 · 21:22 UTCData of 9 November4 min read

The Sella Ronda is a ski tour around the Sella massif in the Dolomites, linking four Ladin valleys by four passes. You can ski it clockwise or counter-clockwise, and it can take up to six hours, so the usual advice is to start before 10 am. Val Gardena, with its villages of Sëlva, Santa Cristina and Urtijëi, is one of the best places to begin. Here is the loop leg by leg, starting from Sëlva in the clockwise direction, with a look at the snow at each height.

Before the first lift: why height decides this tour

Val Gardena is a place where natural snow changes a lot with height, and our record shows it clearly. In our model of 46 past winters, the lowest point of the ski area, at 1,258 m, has a usual peak of only about 25 cm. At 1,882 m it is about 63 cm, and at 2,507 m, the top of our record, about 165 cm. All four passes of the Sella Ronda lie between those last two heights, which is why the loop is a high-mountain day even though it never reaches a summit.

Leg 1: Sëlva to the Gardena Pass

From Sëlva the first climb takes you to the Gardena Pass, at 2,136 m, which links Val Gardena with Corvara in Val Badia. This is the easy, confident start: you are still above the middle of our height range, where a typical winter has the snow you need from January onwards.

Leg 2: Corvara and the Campolongo Pass

Down into Val Badia, then up again over the Campolongo Pass, at 1,875 m, between Corvara and Arabba. This is the lowest of the four passes, almost exactly the height of our middle point. There, a typical winter has about 25 cm of snow in December, 39 cm in January, 48 cm in February and 47 cm in March, and in only 2 weeks of the season are there 50 cm in half of the winters. In other words, this is the leg most exposed to a poor natural-snow year, so ski it with care and expect hard or worn snow late in the day.

Leg 3: Arabba to the Pordoi Pass

From Arabba the route climbs to the Pordoi Pass, at 2,239 m, the highest of the four, and drops down to Canazei in the Fassa valley. Higher up the snow is far more generous: at 2,507 m a typical winter has about 78 cm in January, 92 cm in February and 107 cm in March and April. Strong skiers often find this the best snow of the day.

Leg 4: the Sella Pass and home

The last climb goes over the Sella Pass, at 2,218 m, which connects the Fassa valley with Val Gardena. The final descent back towards Sëlva is where tired legs meet lower snow, so leave time and, if the lower runs look thin, take a lift rather than push on. Anyone going counter-clockwise meets the same passes in reverse: Sella, Pordoi, Campolongo, then Gardena.

When the loop works best

Our numbers point to February and March, when even mid-height has its deepest snow and the top is close to its peak. Natural snow is not the whole story, but it is a fair guide to how much margin you have. The trend is mixed: at 1,882 m the median peak fell from about 79 cm in our first fifteen winters to about 58 cm in the last fifteen, while at 2,507 m it rose from about 165 to 181 cm. Last winter, 2025-26, was poor: about 42 cm at mid-height and 95 cm at the top.

A valley with its own story

Val Gardena is one of five valleys with a majority of Ladin speakers, and it is famous for woodcarving. Racers know it for the Saslong downhill, held almost every year since 1969, and the valley hosted the alpine World Championships in 1970. Before the tour, check which lifts are running on the official website and read the regional avalanche bulletin if you plan to leave the pistes.

Tips for skiers

  • Start before 10 am; the loop can take up to six hours.
  • Watch the Campolongo leg: it is the lowest of the four passes.
  • In a thin year, take a lift down at the end rather than ski worn low runs.
  • Snow on our map does not mean a resort is open: check the lifts on the official website.
