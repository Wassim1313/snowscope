Three day loops from Méribel, the middle of Les 3 Vallées

AlpsGuidesPublished Sunday 8 November 2026 · 19:30 UTCData of 8 November3 min read

Méribel has one great advantage over its neighbours: it sits in the middle. Courchevel lies over the ridge to the east, Les Menuires and Val Thorens over the ridge to the west and south, and together they form Les 3 Vallées, with about 600 km of slopes. From a base in Méribel, at 1,400 m, or in Mottaret, at 1,750 m, each day can head in a different direction. Here are three loops, and what our snow history says about each.

Before you set off: the snow in the middle valley

Méribel's valley has a clear pattern in our model of 46 past winters. At 2,026 m, our mid-mountain point, a typical winter has about 55 cm of snow in December, 95 cm in January, 126 cm in February and 134 cm in March, with a usual peak of about 159 cm. There are at least 50 cm there for 18 weeks in 9 winters out of 10. The valley floor is the weak point: at 1,132 m the usual peak is only about 54 cm. So every loop below starts by going up, and ends with a decision about how to get back down.

Loop 1, east: over the Saulire to Courchevel

The Saulire is the gateway from Méribel to Courchevel. Go early, ski the Courchevel side in the morning while it is quiet, and keep an eye on the clock: the return over the ridge is the one part of the day you cannot skip. This is the loop for confident intermediates who like long, well-groomed runs. Its main risk is not the skiing but the timing, so plan the way home before lunch.

Loop 2, south and west: towards Les Menuires and Val Thorens

This is the high loop. Heading into the third valley takes you to the highest ground of the whole area, where the snow lasts longest. Our own record for Méribel's ski area shows how much height counts: at 2,920 m, the top of our record, the usual peak is about 343 cm, and in April a typical winter still has about 297 cm, the deepest month of the season. It is a long day with many lifts, best on a clear, calm day; in strong wind the high links can close, and the way back is long.

Loop 3, at home: Méribel's own slopes and Mont Vallon

Some days are best spent close to base: after fresh snow, in poor visibility, or simply when the legs are tired. Méribel alone has 76 marked pistes, and the Mont Vallon gondola is one of its biggest lifts. This is also the loop for mixed groups, because everyone can meet at Mottaret for lunch. It has a history too: the women's alpine skiing events of the 1992 Olympic Games were held here.

When to go, and what has changed

For the surest snow at mid-mountain, our numbers point to February and March; the first snow usually reaches 2,026 m around 11 October and the last melts around 15 June, so the high loops can work well into spring. The trend is down: the median peak at that height fell from about 172 cm in our first fifteen winters to about 154 cm in the last fifteen, and the days with snow on the ground from about 241 to 217. Last winter was slightly above normal there, with a peak of about 174 cm.

A note on the founder

Méribel was developed by a Scot: in 1938 the first lift was built above Les Allues, and chalets and hotels followed a year later. Before you book, check lift openings on the official websites, and read the Météo-France avalanche bulletin before any off-piste run between the valleys.

Tips for skiers

  • Plan the return over the ridge before lunch on the Courchevel loop.
  • Keep the long high loop for a clear, calm day.
  • On bad-visibility days stay on Méribel's own slopes and meet at Mottaret.
  • Snow on our map does not mean a resort is open: check the lifts on the official website.
