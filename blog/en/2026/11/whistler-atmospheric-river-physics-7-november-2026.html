A river in the sky over Whistler: why the top gets a metre of snow and the base gets rain

Western CanadaThe physics behind the weatherSnow forecastsPublished Sunday 8 November 2026 · 17:40 UTCData of 7 November4 min read

A train of Pacific storms, fed by an atmospheric river, hits the Coast Mountains from 7 to 12 November. Our forecast shows about 103 cm of new snow at Whistler Blackcomb by 10 November, but the 0 °C level swings between about 1,410 and 2,920 m: deep snow up high, rain at the base, and a warm spell on Wednesday 11 November.

The numbers

On Whistler's upper slopes (2,284 m) our forecast of 7 November gives about 192 mm of water from 7 to 12 November: 56 mm on Sunday 8, 21 mm on Tuesday 10, 40 mm on Wednesday 11 and 73 mm on Thursday 12. For the 72 hours to 10 November our snow forecast gives +103 cm at Whistler Blackcomb, +99 cm at Ski Callaghan and +74 cm at Mount Cain on Vancouver Island, with the snow line near 1,300 m.

Down at sea level the same storms mean rain: about 135 mm in Vancouver over the six days, and up to 17 °C on Wednesday 11 November.

A river in the sky

An atmospheric river is a long, narrow corridor of air, low in the atmosphere, that carries a huge amount of water vapour, usually just ahead of a cold front. Meteorologists measure it as the flow of vapour carried by the wind: above 250 kg of water per metre per second it counts as an atmospheric river, above 750 as a strong one.

Off Vancouver Island we estimate this flow at about 946 on Saturday 7 November and about 987 on Wednesday 11 November, and above 250 for 95 hours of the six days. The jet stream points straight at the coast: at about 3 km up the wind over the ocean reaches 132 km/h, from the south-west.

Why the Coast Mountains squeeze out so much

When this wet air reaches the Coast Mountains it is forced to rise. Rising air expands and cools, its vapour condenses into cloud and falls out as rain or snow. That is orographic lift, and it explains the gradient in our numbers: about 109 mm over the ocean off Vancouver Island, 135 mm in Vancouver, 192 mm on Whistler's upper slopes.

Once the air has crossed the main ridges it has lost much of its water and sinks again, which is why valleys further inland, in the rain shadow, get far less.

Snow at the top, rain at the base: the snow line

Air cools with height, about 6.5 °C per km in the standard atmosphere; in this moist air we find about +3 °C at 1,400 m and about -4 °C at 3,000 m during the wet hours. The 0 °C level is where the air crosses freezing. Snowflakes falling below it start to melt and need a few hundred metres to melt completely, so the snow line sits about 300 m below the 0 °C level.

That level moves a lot from day to day: 1,310 to 1,680 m on Monday 9, 1,500 to 2,430 m on Sunday 8, then 2,390 to 2,920 m on Wednesday 11, when the warm air of the atmospheric river arrives, and 1,730 to 2,490 m on Thursday 12. Weighted by the precipitation, the 0 °C level is near 2,090 m and the snow line near 1,790 m; about 88 % of the water that falls on the upper mountain should fall as snow. The base at 675 m sees rain almost all the time.

On Wednesday the 0 °C level climbs to or above the summits: expect rain or very wet snow high up, before colder air brings snow back on Thursday.

What it means for skiers

Snow from an atmospheric river is heavy and wet: it builds a solid base quickly, but rain and warmth on Wednesday will make a crust at lower and middle heights, buried by new snow on Thursday. Rapid loading plus warming is a classic recipe for rising avalanche danger.

Forecasts change, especially the height of the snow line. Check the snow map, and read Avalanche Canada's forecast before going out of bounds.

What it means for you

  • The best snow will be high: above about 1,800 m the storm falls mostly as snow, below it mostly as rain.
  • Wednesday 11 November is the warm day: rain or wet snow even high up; the colder snow returns on Thursday.
  • Expect a crust under the new snow at middle heights after Wednesday.
  • Out of bounds: always read the official avalanche forecast first; heavy loading and warming raise the danger.

About these numbers

Numbers: forecasts of 7 November 2026 for 7 to 12 November (precipitation, temperature and wind at several heights on Whistler's upper slopes, in Vancouver and over the ocean off Vancouver Island) and the SnowScope 72-hour snow forecast of the same day. We computed the 0 °C level from the temperature profile and took the snow line about 300 m below it; the moisture flow is estimated from wind, temperature and humidity at eight heights. Forecasts change.
