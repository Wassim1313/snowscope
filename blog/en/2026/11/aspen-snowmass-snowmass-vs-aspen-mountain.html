Snowmass or Aspen Mountain? Comparing snow, terrain and who each suits

US WestGuidesPublished Monday 9 November 2026 · 15:41 UTCData of 9 November3 min read

Aspen Snowmass is four ski areas under one pass, and most visitors end up choosing between the two biggest names: Snowmass, the large family mountain, and Aspen Mountain, the steep hill above the town. This guide puts them side by side, with published facts and our snow history of 46 past winters (a model, not resort reports).

Size and shape

Snowmass is the giant: its top is at 12,510 ft (3,810 m) and its base at 8,104 ft (2,470 m), a vertical drop of 4,406 ft (1,343 m) over 3,362 acres, larger than the other three areas combined. It lies about 9 miles (14 km) from downtown Aspen.

Aspen Mountain is compact and steep: from 7,945 ft (2,422 m) to 11,212 ft (3,417 m), a vertical of 3,267 ft (996 m) over 673 acres, with no beginner runs at all. The Silver Queen Gondola leaves from the heart of downtown, so you can walk from your hotel to the lift.

Who each one suits

Snowmass suits mixed groups and families: wide groomed runs, long cruising slopes, and enough steeper terrain higher up for strong skiers. Aspen Mountain suits confident intermediates and experts who want short lift rides and sustained pitches, and visitors who like to ski back into town. Beginners belong at Snowmass, or at the smaller beginner areas on the same pass.

The snow, side by side

Both mountains get the same storms, but Snowmass reaches higher. In our model the usual peak of the season at mid-mountain is about 75 in (190 cm) at Snowmass, around 10,315 ft (3,144 m), and about 69 in (174 cm) on Aspen Mountain, around 9,606 ft (2,928 m). Near the top it is about 91 in (232 cm) at Snowmass, at about 12,516 ft (3,815 m), and about 83 in (210 cm) on Aspen Mountain, at about 11,257 ft (3,431 m).

Month by month at mid-mountain, Snowmass usually has about 33 in (83 cm) in December, 47 in (119 cm) in January, 57 in (146 cm) in February and 66 in (167 cm) in March; Aspen Mountain about 29 in (73 cm), 43 in (109 cm), 53 in (135 cm) and 61 in (154 cm). In 9 of 10 winters, mid-mountain had at least 20 in (50 cm) on the ground for 18 weeks of the season at Snowmass and 17 weeks on Aspen Mountain: a small edge for the higher mountain.

What has changed

Comparing the first 15 winters of the record with the last 15, the usual peak at mid-mountain fell from about 80 in (203 cm) to about 72 in (182 cm) at Snowmass, and from about 72 in (182 cm) to about 67 in (169 cm) on Aspen Mountain. Near the top of Snowmass the drop is larger, from about 95 in (242 cm) to about 83 in (210 cm). Last winter, 2025-26, the peak at mid-mountain on Snowmass reached about 54 in (137 cm), well below the usual.

Getting there and staying

Aspen/Pitkin County Airport lies about three miles northwest of downtown Aspen, so a short transfer gets you to either mountain. Stay in Aspen for restaurants and walking access to Aspen Mountain, or in Snowmass Village for ski-in, ski-out beds on the big mountain; buses link the two.

Avalanche safety

Inside both areas, patrol closures protect you after storms. The Elk Mountains beyond the boundaries are serious avalanche terrain: before any backcountry trip, read the Colorado Avalanche Information Center forecast and carry a transceiver, probe and shovel.

Tips for skiers

  • Families and beginners: base the trip at Snowmass.
  • Experts short on time: Aspen Mountain gives steep runs and short lift rides from town.
  • For the most reliable cover, plan February or March and ski high at Snowmass.
  • Read the CAIC forecast before leaving the ski area boundary.
