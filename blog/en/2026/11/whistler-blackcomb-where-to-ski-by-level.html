Whistler Blackcomb by level: where to ski, from first turns to alpine bowls

Western CanadaGuidesPublished Wednesday 11 November 2026 · 18:31 UTCData of 11 November3 min read

Whistler Blackcomb is two big mountains joined by a gondola, and the right plan depends on how you ski. This tour goes level by level, then adds the snow you can expect at each height from our snow history of 46 past winters (a model, not resort reports).

The two mountains at a glance

Whistler Mountain rises to 2,182 m and Blackcomb to 2,240 m, with the valley bases at 653 m (Creekside) and 675 m (the village). That gives a vertical drop of 1,530 m on Whistler and 1,565 m on Blackcomb, over 3,307 ha of skiable terrain. The Peak 2 Peak Gondola links them high above the valley: 4.4 km long, with a free span of 3.02 km.

First turns and early days

New skiers should not stay in the village at the bottom. The gentle learning areas and green runs are higher up, where the snow is better and the slopes are wide. Take the gondola up, spend the morning on easy terrain near the mid-stations, and ride the lift back down at the end of the day: the lowest runs to the valley are often the hardest snow of the mountain, icy in the morning and soft and wet in the afternoon.

Confident intermediates

This is the level that gets the most out of Whistler Blackcomb. Long blue runs wind down from the alpine to the trees on both mountains, and the Peak 2 Peak lets you ski one mountain in the morning and the other in the afternoon. On a cloudy day, stay in the trees, where the light is better; on a clear day, the open alpine bowls above the tree line are the reward.

Experts and the alpine

The high alpine of both mountains holds bowls, chutes and glacier terrain. These are reached by the upper lifts and some short hikes, and they are often closed after storms until the ski patrol has finished avalanche control. Patience pays: the best days come when a storm clears and the high terrain opens one area after another.

Snow by height and month

Our model gives a deep, long season. At mid-mountain, around 1,456 m, the usual depth is about 100 cm in November, 196 cm in December, 259 cm in January, 299 cm in February and 329 cm in March, with April almost the same at 326 cm. Near the top, around 2,259 m, April is the deepest month, about 430 cm. The usual peak of the season is about 370 cm at mid-mountain and 484 cm near the top.

In 9 of 10 winters, mid-mountain had at least 50 cm on the ground for 32 weeks of the year, and the first snow there usually lies on the ground by about 10 October. Compared with the first 15 winters of the record, the last 15 show a slightly lower peak at mid-mountain, 356 cm instead of 370 cm. The valley floor is a different story: rain is common at the bottom, so judge the snow by the upper mountain.

Getting there and staying

Whistler is 137 km from Vancouver by the Sea-to-Sky Highway, part of Highway 99, which runs along the coast through Squamish. Fly into Vancouver International Airport, then drive or take a coach. The road is mountainous and can be snowy: use winter tyres and check DriveBC before you leave. The pedestrian village at the base has hotels and apartments at every price; Creekside is quieter.

Avalanche safety

Inside the boundary, closures protect you from slopes that are not yet controlled. Beyond it, the Coast Mountains get big storms and heavy snow: read the daily Avalanche Canada forecast for the Sea to Sky region, carry a transceiver, probe and shovel, and travel with trained partners.

Tips for skiers

  • Beginners: learn up the mountain, not at the bottom, and download by lift at the end of the day.
  • For the deepest and most reliable cover, aim for February to April.
  • Check the forecast for the freezing level: rain low down can still mean snow up top.
  • Read the Avalanche Canada forecast before any off-piste or backcountry outing.
