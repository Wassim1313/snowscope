Big Sky in perspective: how big the mountain is, and how its snow changes with height

US WestGuidesPublished Monday 9 November 2026 · 16:57 UTCData of 9 November3 min read

Numbers about Big Sky are easy to quote and hard to picture. Thousands of acres, hundreds of trails, one pyramid-shaped summit. This piece tries to put the size into everyday terms, then shows what our snow history (a model of 46 past winters, not resort reports) says about the snow from the base to the top of Lone Mountain.

The area: a small town of terrain

Big Sky covers about 5,850 acres, or 9.1 square miles (23.7 km²) of skiable terrain. That is the size of a small town, served by 40 lifts and more than 320 trails. In practice it means you can ski for a week and rarely repeat a run, and that the far corners of the area can be a long way from where you started. Plan your last lift back to your base before lunch, not at 3 pm.

The height: one summit above everything

The top of Lone Mountain stands at 11,166 ft (3,403 m), and the resort's vertical drop is about 4,350 ft (1,330 m). Picture three or four tall office towers stacked on top of each other, then cover them with snow. The Lone Peak Tram, with two cabins of up to 75 people, carries skiers to the very top; below it, the mountain spreads out in bowls, glades and long groomed runs.

The snow: base and top are two different worlds

Height changes everything. In our model, the base at 6,818 ft (2,078 m) reaches a usual season peak of only about 28 in (71 cm). Mid-mountain, at 8,990 ft (2,740 m), gets about 82 in (208 cm), and near the summit, at 11,161 ft (3,402 m), about 137 in (347 cm): roughly five times as much as at the base. The upper mountain also stays white for longer, with snow on the ground on about 251 days of a typical winter.

The season: long at the top, short at the bottom

At mid-mountain a typical winter has about 34 in (86 cm) in December, 47 in (120 cm) in January, 56 in (141 cm) in February, 61 in (156 cm) in March and 66 in (168 cm) in April, the deepest month. Near the top the snow keeps building into April, at about 111 in (281 cm), and is still about 101 in (256 cm) in May. Mid-mountain keeps at least 20 in (50 cm) for 23 weeks in 9 winters out of 10. The base is the weak point: the lowest runs are best from late January to March.

Big winters and smaller ones

The range is wide. Near the top, the thinnest winter of our record peaked at about 99 in (252 cm) and the deepest at about 276 in (700 cm). Last winter, 2025-26, reached about 114 in (290 cm) there, below the usual level. Over time the snow has thinned: the median peak near the top fell from about 160 in (406 cm) in our first fifteen winters to about 124 in (316 cm) in the last fifteen, and at mid-mountain from about 94 in (238 cm) to about 76 in (194 cm).

Getting there and staying safe

Big Sky was founded by the Montana broadcaster Chet Huntley and opened in December 1973. It lies about 50 miles (80 km) from Bozeman. The upper mountain is steep, exposed alpine terrain: read the Gallatin National Forest Avalanche Center forecast before you go beyond the groomed runs.

Tips for skiers

  • Big area: plan your way back to your base early in the afternoon.
  • For the deepest snow, ski high; the base area is best from late January to March.
  • Spring trips: the upper mountain usually holds its snow into April.
  • Snow on our map does not mean a resort is open: check lifts and the tram on the official website.
