Mad River Glen, Vermont: three ways to ski the single-chair mountain

US NortheastGuidesPublished Monday 9 November 2026 · 15:08 UTCData of 9 November3 min read

Mad River Glen is small, old-fashioned and proud of it: a cooperative-owned ski area in Vermont where the snow is mostly natural and the most famous lift seats one person. Here are three ways to ski it, with the published facts of the area and our snow history of 46 past winters (a model, not resort reports).

What makes it different

Mad River Glen is the only cooperatively owned ski area in America: skiers buy shares and vote on its decisions. It is one of three US ski areas that do not allow snowboards, and its single chair, built in 1948, is one of only two left in the country. The base is at 1,600 ft (490 m), the top at 3,637 ft (1,109 m), a vertical drop of 2,037 ft (621 m) on about 60 trails. Snowmaking covers only 15% of the terrain, so natural snow decides most of the season. The motto says it plainly: Ski It If You Can.

Way one: the classic day on the single chair

Start with the single chair to the top and the narrow, winding trails cut decades ago, with their bumps, ledges and turns that follow the mountain rather than a bulldozer. This is terrain for confident skiers. Take it slowly the first time, and use the lower lifts to warm up if the snow is hard; Vermont mornings can be icy after a thaw.

Way two: the storm day in the trees

When a storm has just passed, the woods between the trails hold the softest snow. Our model gives the usual peak of the season as about 30 in (75 cm) at mid-mountain, around 2,638 ft (804 m), and about 35 in (90 cm) near the top, around 3,629 ft (1,106 m), so tree skiing needs a real storm or a deep base. Ski with a partner, keep in sight of each other and avoid the trees when the cover is thin: rocks, stumps and streams are close below the surface.

Way three: the safe-snow week in February

If you plan ahead, plan for February. In our model the usual depth at mid-mountain is about 7 in (17 cm) in December, 13 in (32 cm) in January, 20 in (51 cm) in February and 19 in (47 cm) in March, and by April it is almost gone, about 2 in (4 cm). Near the top February and March keep about 25 in (63 cm) and 24 in (61 cm). The first snow on the ground at mid-mountain usually comes around 13 November and the last goes around 20 April.

Natural snow is never certain here. At mid-mountain, a cover of 20 in (50 cm) was reached in half of the winters for only 6 weeks of the season, and near the top for 9; even in the best week, early March, near the top, it was there in 70% of winters. The season peak ranged from about 9 in (22 cm) in the leanest of the 46 winters to about 49 in (125 cm) in the deepest. Interestingly, the recent winters are not worse in our model: the usual peak at mid-mountain moved from about 23 in (58 cm) in the first 15 winters of the record to about 27 in (69 cm) in the last 15. Last winter, 2025-26, peaked at about 26 in (67 cm).

Getting there, staying and safety

Mad River Glen is in Fayston, in Vermont's Mad River Valley, where inns and rentals in the small towns nearby are the usual base. Drive carefully on winter mountain roads and check conditions before you leave. There is no avalanche forecast center for these hills, but steep, wooded terrain has its own risks: stay within the area, follow closures and never ski the trees alone. If you travel to bigger mountains in the US, avalanche.org lists the official forecast centers.

Tips for skiers

  • Plan your trip for February: it is the deepest month in our snow history.
  • Check recent snowfall before you go: with little snowmaking, natural snow decides the day.
  • Ski the trees only after a real storm, and always with a partner.
  • Bring skis, not a snowboard: Mad River Glen is skiers only.
