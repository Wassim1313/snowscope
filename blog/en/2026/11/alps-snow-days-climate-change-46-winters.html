Climate change and Alpine snow: fewer snow days at every height since 1980-81

AlpsGuidesPublished Monday 9 November 2026 · 11:38 UTCData of 9 November3 min read

The Alps still get big winters, but the snow season is shorter. In our 46-winter record at 36 resorts, every one of the 108 points, from 667 m to 3,872 m, lost snow days, and the loss is statistically clear at 81 % of them.

Below 1,500 m: the season shrinks first

Low slopes sit closest to the rain-snow limit. Here the middle point went from 174 snow days a winter (first 15 winters) to 153 (last 15), a drop of 12 %, and the deepest snow of the season from 52 cm to 44 cm, a drop of 16 %. The decline in snow days is clear at 77 % of these points, the drop in peak depth at 43 %: the clearest peak-depth signal of any height.

1,500 to 2,500 m: where most pistes are

Between 1,500 and 2,000 m snow days fell by 7 % (212 to 197) and the usual peak from 97 cm to 88 cm. Between 2,000 and 2,500 m they fell by 8 % (234 to 214), clearly at 90 % of the points, and the peak from 155 cm to 139 cm. The typical trend at these heights is a loss of about 6 to 7 snow days per decade.

Above 2,500 m: deep snow, but a shorter season

High up, a winter still brings well over two metres at the peak: the usual maximum went from 277 cm to 258 cm. The season itself shortened more: from 271 to 240 snow days, 11 %, and the decline is clear at 94 % of the high points. Snow arrives later in autumn and, above all, melts earlier in spring.

Recent winters in context

The poorest winter of our series is 2006-07, with a median of 158 snow days across the points; 2024-25 and 2025-26 follow with 174 each. The richest were 1980-81 (237), 2003-04 (236) and 1990-91 (234). Across all points the typical trend is a loss of about 7.0 snow days and 4.3 cm of peak depth per decade.

What measurements say

Station records tell the same story. Matiu and colleagues analysed snow depth at more than 800 stations in six Alpine countries from 1971 to 2019: on average, seasonal mean depth fell by 8.4 % per decade, seasonal maximum depth and snow cover duration by 5.6 % per decade, with the strongest trends where the snow-to-bare transition happens. In Switzerland, Marty found a step-like drop in snow days at the end of the 1980s at 34 stations between 200 and 1,800 m, a loss of 20 % to 60 %, linked to a jump in winter temperature. Klein and colleagues showed that the Swiss snow season has shortened since 1970 mainly because snow melts earlier in spring.

Our record starts in 1980-81, after part of that shift, and comes from a model rather than stations; that is why the two sets of numbers are not identical, but they point the same way.

What this means for a ski trip

The numbers do not say that the Alps are running out of snow. A typical high resort still has snow on the ground for most of the year, and big winters keep coming. What has changed is the margin: the early-season weeks before Christmas and the late weeks after Easter are less reliable than in the 1980s, and low resorts depend more on snowmaking and on the luck of each winter. Planning around height and slope orientation matters more than it used to.

Tips for skiers

  • Before Christmas and after mid-April, pick high resorts and north-facing slopes.
  • In low resorts, ski in the heart of winter and watch the live map.
  • Check each resort's past-winters chart on SnowScope before you book.
  • Off-piste, read the official avalanche bulletin of the country (EAWS lists them all).

About these numbers

Our numbers: the SnowScope snow history, 46 past winters (1980-81 to 2025-26) read at the base, middle and top of each resort: ERA5-Land, a global reconstruction of past weather, corrected for height. It is a model of past winters, an estimate for an area, not station measurements. Snow day = at least 5 cm on the ground. Change = median of the last 15 winters against the first 15; trend = robust (Theil-Sen) line through all 46 winters, 'clear' when the Mann-Kendall test gives p < 0.05.
