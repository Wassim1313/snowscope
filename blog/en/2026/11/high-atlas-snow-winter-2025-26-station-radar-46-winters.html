High Atlas snow in winter 2025-26: what a station, a radar satellite and 46 winters tell us

Atlas (Morocco)GuidesPublished Tuesday 10 November 2026 · 16:53 UTCData of 10 November7 min read

After five thin winters, the High Atlas finally got a real one. The automatic station at Oukaïmeden, at 2,650 m, measured 92 cm of snow on 19 January 2026. A radar satellite saw snow across the Tensift mountains, and our snow model ranks the winter third of 46. Here is what each of these sources shows, and where they disagree.

What the station on the plateau measured

The Oukaïmeden station stands on the open plateau below the ski lifts. A sensor looks down from a mast and measures the distance to the snow surface with sound, so the snow depth is the change in that distance. A second sensor on the ground counts natural radiation from the soil: the more water lies on top of it as snow, the weaker the signal. That gives the snow water, the amount of water you would get if you melted the snow.

The record we have starts on 1 January 2026, and there were already 44 cm of snow on the ground that morning. A storm on 5 and 6 January took the depth to 62 cm. The snow then settled for ten days, as fresh snow always does, back to 51 cm on 16 January. Then came the big storm of the winter: from 17 to 19 January the depth rose to 92 cm, the highest value in the record we have. On 20 January the snow held 205 mm of water.

Eighteen days without data

Then the logger stopped. From 22 January to 8 February there is no record at all, a gap of 18 days, just after the deepest snow of the winter. When the station came back on 9 February, the depth was 33 cm. We do not know whether the snow stayed near 90 cm for a while and then melted, or whether more snow fell and melted in between. We have not tried to fill this gap. It is shown as a grey band in the first chart, with a question mark, because that is the honest answer.

After 9 February the melt is clear and fast. The sun is strong at this latitude, the air was mild, and the depth fell almost every day. On 21 February the depth sensor saw bare ground. A short storm brought the snow back on 3 March, up to 17 cm on 5 March, but it was gone within a week. The water sensor still saw a thin layer of snow and ice until mid-March, probably in patches next to the sensor, and read zero from 16 March.

One more detail from the field. From April to August the depth sensor does not see snow, it sees grass and plants growing under the mast. Without a filter, the plateau would look snowy in May. So we set the depth to zero whenever the ground is no longer white and the water sensor reads nothing.

What the radar saw from space

A single station tells you about one spot. To see the whole range we used snow depth estimated from a radar satellite. Radar sees through clouds and works at night, which matters in a winter of storms. Between September and July the satellites passed over the Tensift mountains 110 times, every few days, and each pass gave us a map of the snow depth. To turn depth into water we used the density of the snow from our model.

Over the whole Tensift basin, 13,051 km² from the plains of Marrakech to the summits around Toubkal, the radar shows the snow building quickly at the end of December. The largest snow-covered area, 1,881 km², came on 22 January. The largest amount of water stored in the snow came on 27 January, with about 272 million m³. If the snow was denser than our model assumes, the true value could be as high as 409 million m³. Our snow model, run on the same area, peaks at 230 million m³ on 19 January, the very day the station saw its deepest snow.

Where the radar and the model disagree

The two agree on the winter's build-up and on its size. They disagree about the spring. In the model, the snow went fast: half of the peak was gone by 31 January, and on 1 March only 5 % of it was left. In the radar view, half of it was still there on 9 February, and on 1 March 59 % was left. Snow-cover maps from an optical satellite, which only see whether the ground is white or not, sit in between: on 1 March they still showed 24 % of the largest snow-covered area.

Who is right? Probably neither fully. The model may melt the snow too quickly on high, shaded slopes: with 1 km squares it cannot see a north-facing gully. The radar has its own weakness: when snow is wet it reflects the signal in a different way, and the method has to guess. At the station point the radar was too generous. On the 15 passes with snow at the station or in the radar view, the radar read on average 32 cm more than the sensor on the mast, with a typical error of 42 cm on a single day. Around the station it still showed snow on 16 March, about 3 weeks after the depth sensor saw bare ground.

Part of that is not an error at all. A radar pixel covers a patch of slope, not the flat plateau under one mast. North-facing gullies and wind-blown hollows keep snow much longer than the station's spot. But part of it is the method, and we would not trust a radar number at one place on one day.

Against 46 winters

To place this winter in history we used our snow model for the Tensift mountains above 1,500 m, an area of 3,843 km², over 46 winters from 1980-81 to 2025-26. The model is not a measurement, but it treats every winter the same way, which is what a comparison needs.

In winter 2025-26 the snow in this area held 216 million m³ of water at its peak, on 19 January. That is the third-highest value in 46 winters, after 1990-91 (263) and 1984-85 (241), and about 2.9 times the usual peak, the middle value of all winters, which is 74. The five winters before it had peaks between 18 and 86, and 2023-24 was the poorest of the whole record. After a run like that, a winter close to the top of the list matters for the rivers below, and for the wells and reservoirs of the plain.

What each source can and cannot tell

The station is the only real measurement. It is precise at one point and one height, but it saw nothing before 1 January, nothing during 18 key days, and it says little about the summits 1,000 m higher.

The radar covers the whole range through cloud. It is good at showing where the snow is and how the season built up. It is weak on the exact depth at one place and in wet spring snow, so its spring numbers come with a wide margin.

The model gives the long view: 46 winters treated alike. It agrees with the station on the timing of the peak, but in spring it melts the snow faster than both satellites suggest. Read together, the three tell a consistent story: a big winter, peaking in the second half of January, followed by a fast melt at middle heights and a slower one on the high, shaded slopes.

How sure are we?

Sure about the big picture: this was a large winter, far above the five before it. Fairly sure about the timing: all three sources put the peak between 19 and 27 January. Less sure about the amount of water, which depends on the density of the snow, and least sure about how long the snow lasted in spring, where the radar and the model are weeks apart.

Tips for skiers

  • In the Atlas, one or two storms can make a winter: watch the forecast for the big systems from the Atlantic rather than the monthly averages.
  • At ski-area height the snow can go within two or three weeks of the last storm; go soon after a big snowfall.
  • North-facing slopes and gullies above 3,000 m keep snow much longer than the plateau; they are also where avalanches and hard, icy snow are most likely.
  • Before any trip off the pistes, check the latest weather bulletin and ask local mountain guides about conditions.

About these numbers

Station: Oukaïmeden automatic weather station (2,650 m, 31.2065 N, 7.8663 W), sonic snow-depth sensor (SR50A) and gamma snow-water sensor (CS725), raw logger files from 1 January 2026; daily median depth after quality and spike filters, bare-ground offset removed, depth set to zero when the ground is snow-free (vegetation echo April-August). Logger gaps: 10-11 January and 22 January-8 February 2026. Radar: Sentinel-1 snow depth (cross-polarisation change method of Lievens et al. 2019, 2022, adapted to the High Atlas; morning passes, 100 m grid), Tensift basin 13,051 km², snow water from depth times the density of our model; range = alternative densities. Optical snow cover: MODIS. Model: our 1 km snow model of Morocco (degree-day snowpack driven by ERA5-Land, downscaled for height), Tensift cells above 1,500 m, 46 water years 1980-81 to 2025-26. All numbers: SnowScope analysis scripts, 10 November 2026.
