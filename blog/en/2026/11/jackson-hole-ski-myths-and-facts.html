Jackson Hole ski myths and facts: snow, steeps and the tram

US WestGuidesPublished Monday 9 November 2026 · 20:02 UTCData of 9 November3 min read

Jackson Hole Mountain Resort has a reputation that arrives before you do: steep, wild, experts only. Some of it is true, some of it is not. Here are six common beliefs, checked against the published facts of the resort and our snow history of 46 past winters (a model, not resort reports).

Myth: it is only for experts

Fact: the steep terrain is real, and it is the reason many people come, but it is not the whole mountain. The resort has 2,500 acres inbounds over a vertical drop of 4,139 ft (1,262 m), from a base at 6,311 ft (1,924 m) to the top of Rendezvous Mountain at 10,450 ft (3,185 m). The lower mountain has gentler slopes where beginners and cautious intermediates can spend good days. The honest advice: intermediates will enjoy it, but should not expect a mountain of easy cruising runs.

Myth: you ski straight from the town of Jackson

Fact: the resort is in Teton Village, about twelve miles northwest of Jackson, and Jackson Hole Airport is about 11 miles northeast of Teton Village. Staying in Teton Village puts you at the lifts; staying in Jackson gives more choice of beds and restaurants, with a drive or bus each morning.

Myth: the snow is gone by spring

Fact: in our model the high mountain keeps its snow late. Near the top, around 10,397 ft (3,169 m), April is the deepest month of a usual winter, about 105 in (267 cm), and the usual peak of the season is about 117 in (297 cm). At mid-mountain, around 8,363 ft (2,549 m), the usual depth is about 36 in (92 cm) in December, 54 in (136 cm) in January, 65 in (165 cm) in February, 71 in (180 cm) in March and still 67 in (169 cm) in April. The lower slopes near 6,329 ft (1,929 m) are the exception: they peak at only about 46 in (116 cm) and melt first.

Myth: climate change has already emptied the mountain

Fact: not at mid-mountain, according to our model. Comparing the first 15 winters of the record with the last 15, the usual peak there is about the same, about 80 in (203 cm) then and 81 in (205 cm) now, although the number of days with snow on the ground fell from 250 to 239. Near the top the peak is slightly lower, about 112 in (285 cm) against 117 in (297 cm). Last winter, 2025-26, peaked at about 64 in (162 cm) at mid-mountain, below the usual. In 9 of 10 winters, mid-mountain had at least 20 in (50 cm) for 22 weeks of the season.

Myth: Corbet's Couloir is the reason to go

Fact: Corbet's Couloir is the resort's most famous and most difficult line, and most visitors only look at it. The better reason to go is the wide range of terrain that holds snow after storms, and the aerial tram, a 100-passenger cabin that opened on 20 December 2008 and replaced the original tram of 1966, which takes you to the top in one ride.

Myth: the backcountry gate means it is safe

Fact: the resort lists about 3,000 acres of backcountry beyond its boundary, and leaving through a gate means leaving patrolled terrain. Read the daily Bridger-Teton Avalanche Center forecast, carry a transceiver, probe and shovel, and go with partners who know the terrain.

Tips for skiers

  • Intermediates: come, but plan your days around the gentler lower and middle mountain.
  • For the deepest snow, aim for February to April; the high mountain holds it longest.
  • Stay in Teton Village to walk to the lifts, or in Jackson for more choice.
  • Never pass a backcountry gate without the avalanche forecast and rescue gear.
